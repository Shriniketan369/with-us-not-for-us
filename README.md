<h1 align="center">With Us, Not For Us</h1>

<p align="center"><em>On learning to code before AI could do it for me, and what working with it has taught me</em></p>

<p align="center">By <strong>Shriniketan Rao Turpu</strong></p>

<br>

---

<br>

My first website was **not** built with AI. I was in 5th or 6th grade, and I made it from textbooks and documentation, one confusing page at a time. **I learned to code before AI could do it for me**, and I think that still shapes how I use it today.

I started using AI at the end of 2023, and by 2024 it had become part of my work. Since then, Claude has been with me through every research project and product I've built. But the way it helped me was never by doing the work alone. **It helped me look at problems from angles I would not have found myself.**

<br>

---

<br>

## The moment that started PromptEcho

The clearest example is where my research began. While working with Claude, I noticed something small: when my instruction said **"ask questions if required,"** the response came out one way, and when it said **"ask questions *only* if required,"** it came out differently. I tried it multiple times, and the difference was real. I even saved the conversation, from June 2026, where I described what I was seeing and asked Claude why it happens.

Claude explained what the word "only" does. But the explanation raised a bigger question for me: **how large is this effect, and how consistent is it?** That question became **PromptEcho**, my research on how LLM outputs drift under small, developer-style edits to a prompt. An observation that most people would have scrolled past became a paper, because I was curious enough to measure it.

## The fear that AI makes us less capable

People often worry that AI will take jobs, or that it will make people less capable. I understand the fear, but my experience points the other way.

I once had an idea for a **crop disease detection system** using computer vision, a field that was completely new to me. AI did not hand me a finished project. **It helped me find a way in.** Later, the same kind of exploration led me into **quantum machine learning with PennyLane**, a subject I would not have approached on my own. AI did not replace what I could do. **It widened what I was willing to try.**

<br>

---

<br>

## The habit that makes it work

I don't hand AI a task and wait for the answer. I **explain the task and the context first**, then ask what it thinks and what the steps would be. I also **tell it to guide me instead of doing the work.**

When I was building the crop disease system, I didn't ask for code. I asked where to get the dataset, what kind of dataset it should be, and why. **I want to own whatever I build**, which means understanding every part well enough to find the bug when something breaks.

<br>

---

<br>

## When AI is wrong

AI hasn't caused me a major setback, but it has been **confidently wrong**. ChatGPT and Claude have both given me outdated or false information in a completely assured tone, and it took four or five messages and real sources before they corrected it.

In 2025 I tried Microsoft Copilot for applications and internships. It was useful, but its sources were old, some answers were inaccurate, and it was less sure of itself. I haven't used it since. What I took from this is that **AI's confidence is not evidence.** Checking it is my job, and that is part of why I always bring the context myself.

<br>

---

<br>

## With us, not for us

I don't think AI has to replace people. I think it is a **companion**: something that helps us see a problem from a new angle, and opens doors to innovation and discovery that we might not have opened alone.

But it only works that way when we bring something to it. When I bring the task, the context, and the curiosity, AI becomes useful. When I don't, it is just a confident voice with no direction.

That is the lesson of everything I've built so far.

<br>

<h3 align="center"><strong>AI works best when it works with us, not for us.</strong></h3>
